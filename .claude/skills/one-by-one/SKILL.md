# One-by-one — one item per response, until the list is done

An item may need a decision, or may just need to be understood, or both. Either
way the user should come out of it genuinely understanding what was in front of
them — that is what the skill is for. Where an item is a decision, it is also an
open discussion: options, trade-offs, and your honest opinion on the table, with
the user deciding. Throughput is never the goal. If covering more ground would
cost the user's understanding, slow down.

Applies to **every response until the list is worked through completely**.

## Step 1 — establish the list

**If the user named the list**, that is the list — don't add adjacent items you
think also deserve attention, don't drop ones that look already-handled. Take it
in the order they gave it, and start on the first item; re-ordering or restating
a list they are already looking at buys nothing and invites confusion.

**Otherwise**, take the most reasonable reading of "all pending items" for this
conversation: whatever is genuinely still unsettled — open questions, undecided
choices, disagreements, review comments, proposed changes. Don't pad it. Lay the
list out before starting on it, ordered so that items others depend on come
first.

If the scope is genuinely ambiguous, give the list you believe is right, name the
other reading, and ask before starting.

## Step 2 — introduce the item, properly, every time

Every item gets a real introduction when its turn comes, **even if it was already
introduced earlier in the conversation** — a frequent reason for invoking this
skill is that the first explanation didn't land. Never point back at earlier text
with "as I mentioned above" or "recapping quickly." Re-introduce it fully, and if
the earlier explanation didn't work, come at it from a different angle rather
than restating the same words.

A good introduction:

- **Says what the item is** before saying anything about what to do about it, and
  stands on its own — assume the user has read nothing else in the conversation.
  Name things; no "the second concern."
- **Skips technical language that isn't earning its place.** Use a term when it is
  the precise thing being discussed, not when a plain sentence does the same work.
  When one is genuinely needed, explain it inline in a clause rather than assuming
  it's known.
- **Explains why it matters** — what's at stake, what each direction costs. An item
  whose stakes the user can't see is one they can't meaningfully agree to.
- **Puts the choice on the table** when there is one: the options, their
  trade-offs, and what you'd pick and why. Your opinion belongs here — as
  something to push against, not as a verdict.

Long enough to do that, and no longer.

## Step 3 — one item per response. Strictly.

**Exactly one item may be the subject of any one response.** No "let's tackle both
X and Y," no "quick note on Z before we continue," no handling a small item in
passing because it seems trivial. If it's on the list, it gets its own turn.
Naming items — laying the list out, or adding one that surfaced along the way — is
not discussing them and doesn't count against this.

Cross-references are fine and often useful: *"the decision we just made to store
timestamps in UTC is what makes this next one straightforward."* The test is
whether the other item is being **discussed and settled** here, or merely
**referred to**. The rule governs what you discuss with the user, not what you
look at — research and verify as much as an item needs.

Follow-ups about the current item are still the current item — answer as many as
the user asks, for as long as they ask. If the user raises a different item
mid-flow, follow them; they set the pace and the order, and the list is your
plan, not a rule binding them.

## Step 4 — the user decides when an item is settled

**You do not close an item. Ever.** When you believe agreement or understanding
has been reached, you *ask* — "I think we're in agreement here, good to move on?"
or "that's the whole picture as I see it, does that land or should I come at it
from another direction?" — then stop and wait. What you must never do is declare
it settled and move on in the same breath; "agreed, so on to the next item" takes
the decision out of the user's hands.

If they say yes, introduce the next item in your next response. If they say no,
or ask more, or push back, stay on it. There is no budget for how many turns an
item may take.

Silence, a vague reply, or the absence of an objection is not agreement. If it's
unclear whether they're satisfied, ask again rather than assuming.

## Step 5 — finishing

When the last item is settled, the skill is off and the session continues as
normal.
